### 3. Opponent Types: Five Categories

Locate each player on loose/tight × aggressive/passive, plus an "unknown" category.

| Type | Identifying traits | Exploitation |
|---|---|---|
| Loose-passive (calling station) | Calls a lot, raises little, calls any price | **Never bluff, never slow-play**. Pure value betting, heavy bets with strong hands |
| Tight-passive (rock) | Low VPIP, folds under pressure | Play loose and steal their pots; fold immediately when they finally bet |
| Loose-aggressive (LAG) | Bets/raises frequently, multi-street bluffs; **but is dangerous precisely when building small pots** | Stop fighting back. Check-call with strong hands and let them self-destruct; give up small pots, win big ones when they're strong |
| Tight-aggressive (TAG) | Low VPIP but bets/raises often, sensible sizing, rarely calls passively | Avoid them, bluff less. **Their postflop call of a big bet almost always means slow-playing a strong hand** |
| Unknown | Fewer than 3 samples of the same pattern | **Judge by the standard line, no exploitative deviation** |

Hard rules:
1. **A single hand does not define a player.** The same pattern must appear 3-4 times to be "exploitable". Below that, classify as "unknown".
2. **"Unknown" is not "weak".** An exploitative deviation against an unknown opponent is itself a gift to them.
3. Every piece of advice must name "which type, exploiting which tendency" — no one-size-fits-all advice.

Six exploitable betting patterns: bets when strong and checks when weak (I bet when they check) / bets when on a draw (bet big on draw-heavy boards) /
continuation-bets after raising preflop to control the pot (call to trap when I have a strong hand in position) / **only one barrel** (they bet the flop,
a turn bet means I'm beaten, a turn check means the pot is mine) / bets heavy when bluffing (I call heavy river bets) /
bets small with weak hands (I raise when they bet small).

### 4. Inferring an Opponent's Hand Range

This is a 2005 book; it teaches "guessing the opponent's cards", not modern combinatorial range-balancing.
The four clues below come from the book; **the range-flavored wording is a reasonable extrapolation** — don't invent range theory the book doesn't have.

**Clue 1 · Position**: the later you enter, the wider your range. An early-position raise = strong range; a late-position raise may be a blind steal.
A CO raise is usually stronger than a BTN raise (BTN steal is already industry standard).

**Clue 2 · Bet sizing**: small sizing = wants a call; large sizing = wants a fold or is protecting a vulnerable hand.
**Abnormal sizing is the biggest tell**, but first confirm whether this opponent is the "small with strong" or "big with strong" type
— the book explicitly says both kinds exist; don't assume.

**Clue 3 · Checking**: by default it signals "missed the flop", but you must distinguish three kinds:
weakness check (ready to fold, I bet and take it) / trap check (strong hand waiting for me to bet, be careful) /
draw check (wants a free card, don't give it). **A check in a multiway pot is more credible than heads-up.**

**Clue 4 · Raising and re-raising**: the book's most direct inference is
— **what a player did NOT do is as informative as what they did**:
a caller usually doesn't have a hand that can re-raise, otherwise they would have raised themselves.

Synthesis order: rough preflop range → street by street watch which parts of their range they keep and which they discard
→ cross-check with board structure (how much of their range did this card help).

**River reversal rule**: played weak earlier then suddenly makes a suspicious big river bet = bluff;
played aggressive earlier then suddenly timid on the river = trap; aggressive and probing = medium hand, unsure.

Forbidden: stating a range as a specific hand ("he has AA"); talking about "his range" with fewer than 3 samples;
strong judgments from a single clue (need at least three clues pointing the same way); reverse-engineering his range from showdown results.
